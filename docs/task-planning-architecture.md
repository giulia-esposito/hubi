# Hubi — Task Planning: A Critical Architectural Review

**Status: architectural review, Version 1. No code, no prompts, no other document's contents were changed to produce this — investigation and reasoning only.** Supersedes the "Response Mode" framing in `docs/response-mode-architecture.md` (kept, marked superseded, not deleted — its evidence is reused here, not redone). Written as a direct critical response to a product challenge: is Hubi still architected as a retrieval system that answers questions, when its actual mission is broader? This document takes a position on that question, and does not simply validate what was proposed — Section 6 states plainly where the proposal, as given, needed correcting.

---

## 1. The question under review

The claim under review, restated precisely: Hubi's implicit flow today is `Question → Retrieve → Reason → Answer`. The proposed flow is `Question → understand job-to-be-done → understand desired outcome → Retrieve governed knowledge → Reason → produce a business artifact`. And: is "Response Mode" (a property of how Reasoning constructs its output) the wrong abstraction, when the real gap is *how Hubi thinks before responding* — implying a new layer, "Task Planning," between Applicability and Reasoning?

Three separate questions are bundled inside that claim, and they don't all have the same answer. Taking them one at a time is the only way to avoid rubber-stamping the whole proposal because part of it is clearly right.

---

## 2. Is Hubi a retrieval system, or a job-to-be-done copilot? — this is not a new pivot

Before treating this as a proposed change, it's worth checking whether it already *is* the documented vision and the implementation simply hasn't caught up. It is:

- **`CLAUDE.md`** (the org-level, slow-changing philosophy document, present since before any code was written): *"Hubi is not simply a chatbot... Users should never need to know where documentation lives... Instead, they describe what they are trying to accomplish. Hubi figures out the rest."*
- **`Product_Architecture.md` Section 1**: the stated long-term vision already lists *"prepare for customer conversations," "navigate complex scenarios," "receive contextual guidance," "support decision making"* — alongside "retrieve trusted knowledge," not instead of it.

**This reframing is not a new pivot in product thinking. It's a restatement of the founding vision, and the finding here is that the *architecture and the running system* have not caught up to it — not that the vision was wrong.** That distinction matters for how this gets treated: it's not "we got the philosophy wrong and need to fix it," it's "we deliberately shipped a narrower first slice of an always-broader vision, and real testing has now told us the narrower slice has reached its limit."

That "deliberately" is important and verifiable, not assumed: `Prototype_Plan.md` Section 6 records, in plain language, that *"scope was deliberately kept narrow, per explicit product direction: validate session continuity, context reuse, clarifying questions, grounded citations, and safe refusal — without building a general request-classification framework."* This was a reasonable, low-risk way to validate the hardest part first (can Hubi ground answers safely at all) before building the harder, more expansive part (can Hubi help accomplish work). **The Response Mode investigation and this one are the first concrete evidence that the hardest part is validated enough to justify building the next part.** That's a healthy sequencing outcome, not an architectural failure to correct defensively.

## 3. Critical evaluation of the proposed flow reordering

**The current flow is exactly `Question → Retrieve → Reason → Answer`, confirmed at the code level, not assumed:** `app/api/chat/route.ts` calls `search()` on the raw accumulated conversation text *before* any understanding of what the user is trying to accomplish exists anywhere in the system. There is no step, anywhere, that asks "what is this for" before retrieving.

**This has a concrete technical cost, not just a philosophical one — and it's already been observed, not hypothesized.** Finding #003's reproduction is the clearest evidence: the question *"I have a new Enterprise client in Mexico. Which onboarding meetings do I need to run?"* retrieved five irrelevant chunks about SMB contract paperwork, because BM25 searched the raw sentence, with no understanding that the user's actual job-to-be-done was *"get the Enterprise onboarding meeting sequence for a Mexico-based client."* A retrieval query informed by that understanding — even something as simple as "Enterprise onboarding meeting sequence" — would very plausibly have found `RKC-000006` directly. **This means fixing "how Hubi thinks before responding" isn't only about business-assistance requests (Category C from the prior investigation) — it would very likely also help retrieval quality for ordinary factual questions, which is a stronger, more urgent case for this reordering than the original NotebookLM comparison alone.**

**Where I disagree with the proposed placement, precisely:** the proposed flow puts the new understanding step (`Task Planning`) *after* Applicability, immediately before Reasoning — i.e., after retrieval has already happened. That position can only ever fix how Reasoning *constructs* its output from whatever was retrieved. It cannot fix *what gets retrieved* in the first place — which is exactly the failure Finding #003 demonstrates and exactly the kind of failure a job-aware retrieval query would help with. **Placing this understanding step only downstream of retrieval solves half the problem the evidence points to and leaves the other half untouched.**

**Recommendation: the understanding step needs to happen before retrieval, not only before Reasoning — one understanding, computed once, consumed twice.** This doesn't require two separate mechanisms. It requires the same job-to-be-done understanding to be available to both the Knowledge Engine (to shape or supplement the retrieval query) and to Reasoning (to shape construction latitude) — the same pattern the architecture already uses for Business/Task Context, which is established once and consumed by both the Applicability Layer and Reasoning without being recomputed at each stage.

**Does moving this step before Applicability violate "governance always precedes reasoning"? No — and this is worth stating precisely, not just asserted.** The non-negotiable principle is that *Reasoning* — the mechanism that composes an answer from knowledge — never operates before governance/applicability has filtered that knowledge. Understanding *what the user is asking for* is not an operation on knowledge at all; it's an operation on the user's own words, exactly like Request Classification and Context Builder already are in the documented architecture (both of which already run before Applicability, uncontroversially). A job-to-be-done understanding step is the same category of thing. The invariant that must hold, stated as a hard boundary: **this step may never see, use, or reason over retrieved content — only the user's own message and conversation history.** Once that's true, its position ahead of retrieval and Applicability is safe by construction, not by exception.

**Does using this understanding to shape retrieval conflict with "Hubi retrieves broadly but answers narrowly"?** Only if it *replaces* the broad pass rather than *supplementing* it. The correct shape, consistent with a pattern this project has already designed elsewhere: job-to-be-done understanding should trigger an *additional*, targeted retrieval pass when the broad pass looks insufficient — the same conditional-expansion shape already specified in `docs/context-applicability-architecture.md` Section 6 for multilingual queries (cheap sufficiency check → conditional expansion → graceful fallback to the original results). This is not a new retrieval mechanism; it's the same mechanism, triggered by a new, richer signal.

## 4. Is a new layer actually missing, or does one already exist and was never built out?

The proposed flow (`Intent → Context → Applicability → Task Planning → Reasoning → Artifact`) treats Task Planning as a wholly new layer. Checked against `Architecture.md`, that's not quite right:

- **Section 2 (Conversation Orchestrator)** already assigns exactly this responsibility: *"determining what kind of assistance the user needs... deciding whether additional context is required."*
- **Section 3 (Request Classification)** is the closest existing attempt at operationalizing that responsibility — four types (Direct Question, Business Scenario, Decision Support, Process Guidance) — but, as the prior investigation found, none of the four explicitly authorize constructing a work product, and the taxonomy has never been built as code (confirmed: no classification logic exists anywhere in the repository).

**Conclusion: this is not a missing layer so much as an already-assigned responsibility that was scoped too thinly and never built.** Framing it as "Task Planning, an entirely new layer" risks treating this as a bigger architectural departure than it actually is, which matters for how much re-justification this needs against the rest of the standing architecture. Framing it instead as *"Request Classification, finally given the depth its own job-to-be-done examples always implied, and finally built"* is the more accurate, more conservative, and more defensible description — and it changes the recommendation: **rather than replacing all four existing request types, Task Planning should deepen the one type that was always a catch-all** — Business Scenario — into an explicit job-to-be-done taxonomy (learn something, make a decision, prepare for a meeting, write/tailor a document, create discovery questions, review existing work, generate a business artifact, etc.), while leaving Direct Question and Process Guidance as-is (their expected behavior was already unambiguous and matches what's actually needed). **Decision Support sits at the boundary and needs a real product decision, not an assumption** — some Decision Support requests ("Can I offer a 60% discount?") are Category-A-strict facts-and-policy questions in conversational disguise; others plausibly shade toward the same job-to-be-done richness as Business Scenario. This is flagged as an open decision in Section 9, not resolved here.

## 5. Is "Response Mode" the wrong abstraction? — revising my own prior recommendation

Yes, on reflection, agreeing with the challenge — and it's worth being explicit about *why*, since the point isn't just to agree, it's to show the reasoning holds up.

`docs/response-mode-architecture.md` defined Response Mode as an independent classification, assigned *after* the fact, requiring a manually-authored lookup table mapping each Request Classification type to a Response Mode (Direct Question → Grounded Fact, Decision Support → Grounded Synthesis, and so on). That table is a symptom of getting the causality backwards: **construction latitude isn't an independent fact about a request — it's a *consequence* of what the user is actually trying to accomplish.** Once a job-to-be-done understanding exists (Section 4), the appropriate construction latitude falls out of it directly; it doesn't need to be separately classified and then reconciled against a second taxonomy.

**Recommendation: retire "Response Mode" as an independently-tracked concept.** Fold its meaning into Task Planning's output directly — each job-to-be-done category has a documented default construction latitude (a property of the category, not a second classification a human or model has to perform separately). This is a simplification, not just a rename: one axis to reason about instead of two, consistent with this project's own stated engineering preference (`CLAUDE.md`: *"when two solutions provide similar value, choose the simpler one"*).

The three latitude *levels* themselves (Grounded Fact / Grounded Synthesis / Grounded Assistance) hold up and should carry forward unchanged as the vocabulary for what each job-to-be-done category permits — only the idea that they need their own independent classification step is being retired.

## 6. Where the proposal, as given, needs correcting — stated plainly, per the instruction not to simply agree

1. **Cost and latency.** A job-to-be-done understanding step that runs on every single turn is a new, likely-mandatory addition to the reasoning cost of every request — in tension with the explicit principle that *"AI capabilities with real per-use cost are invoked only when needed, not by default"* (`Product_Architecture.md` Section 2). This review takes no position on the engineering mechanism (that's implementation, out of scope here), but the architecture-level requirement should be stated now, while it's still cheap to state: whatever eventually implements this must not silently become a second unconditional model round-trip per turn. Whether that means folding the understanding step into the same reasoning-engine call that already happens, or something else, is an implementation decision to make later — but the *constraint* is architectural and belongs here.
2. **A richer taxonomy has more boundary risk, not less.** Nine job-to-be-done categories create more category boundaries to get wrong than today's four request types — and the project has already paid once for a boundary-drawn-wrong mistake (Finding #002's unstated-region-as-default). The Decision-Support/Business-Scenario boundary flagged in Section 4, and the objection-handling/negotiation-prep boundary flagged in the prior investigation (`docs/response-mode-architecture.md` Section 2), don't go away because the concept was renamed — renaming doesn't fix a boundary problem, only careful category design does. Recommendation: start with a small number of coarse job clusters, not the full nine-item list at once, mirroring this project's own "smallest V0 boundary" discipline (`docs/context-applicability-architecture.md` Section 10).
3. **The proposed placement (between Applicability and Reasoning) under-solves the problem the evidence actually supports.** Addressed in Section 3 — corrected to run before retrieval as well.
4. **"Task Planning" as a wholly new layer over-states the departure from the existing architecture.** Addressed in Section 4 — corrected to a deepening of an already-assigned, already-partially-designed responsibility.

None of these four corrections argue against the proposal's direction. They argue for building it as a deliberate extension of what's already documented, at a smaller and better-bounded first scope than "nine categories, running on every turn, as a brand-new layer."

## 7. Revisiting Hubi's positioning against NotebookLM

*"NotebookLM helps users work with documents. Hubi should help Revenue employees work with governed organizational knowledge. Knowledge retrieval is only one capability inside that broader mission."* This is accurate, and — like Section 2's finding — it's largely a restatement of something already established rather than a new insight: `Product_Architecture.md` Section 1 already frames Hubi as governed by the Registry and generating Knowledge Intelligence back to it, a relationship NotebookLM's document model has no equivalent of at all (no eligibility field, no lifecycle/Content Status, no region/audience applicability, no accountable owner). The differentiation from NotebookLM was already conceptually complete before this conversation — what Task Planning changes is not *whether* Hubi is differentiated from NotebookLM, but *how much Hubi can now do* while staying differentiated.

**The one thing worth stating as an explicit, permanent guarantee, precisely because broadening Hubi's mission makes it worth restating out loud:** expanding *what Hubi can help accomplish* must never expand *what knowledge Hubi is allowed to use while accomplishing it*. Task Planning governs construction latitude over already-eligible, already-applicable knowledge — it has no authority over eligibility or applicability themselves, and no version of this proposal should ever grant it that authority. This is the same invariant `docs/response-mode-architecture.md` stated for Response Mode; it carries forward unchanged as the actual boundary that keeps Hubi's broader mission from becoming NotebookLM with extra steps and no governance.

## 8. What changes, what doesn't

**Changes:**
- A job-to-be-done understanding step is added, positioned after Context Builder and before the Knowledge Engine — deepening Request Classification's Business Scenario type (and, pending a product decision, parts of Decision Support) rather than replacing all four existing types.
- Its output is consumed twice: to optionally supplement retrieval (an additional, conditional pass — same shape as the already-designed multilingual expansion), and to determine Reasoning's construction latitude.
- "Response Mode" is retired as an independently-classified concept; its three latitude levels survive as a property derived from job-to-be-done, not a second classification.
- `Architecture.md` Section 11's Response Model (Direct Answer, Options, Process Steps, Sources, etc.) will eventually need a new element type for a generated work product (a drafted pitch, email, or question set) — flagged here, not designed here.
- The terminal concept in `Architecture.md`'s own high-level diagram ("Answer · Sources · Materials · Next Step") will eventually need to read as "Answer or Artifact" — a small, deliberate naming call for Product, not made here.

**Does not change:**
- The Applicability Layer's position, logic, or authority — Task Planning has zero power over eligibility or applicability, by design (Section 7).
- "Governance always precedes reasoning" — preserved exactly, because the new step classifies the *request*, never the *knowledge* (Section 3).
- The three-state eligible → applicable → grounded backbone (`Product_Architecture.md` Section 5).
- Citation and traceability requirements — a constructed artifact still must trace every factual claim to grounded knowledge, with zero exception, exactly as `docs/response-mode-architecture.md` specified.
- The Registry-as-source-of-truth relationship, and Hubi's role as a consumer of governance, never a creator of it.
- Conversation Continuity's architecture — an entirely separate, already-designed concern, untouched by any of this.
- Findings #001/#002/#003 — none of their fixes are superseded, delayed, or reframed by this; they remain the retrieval/governance track, proceeding independently.

2. What is the smallest, coarsest first version of the job-to-be-done taxonomy worth building — full nine categories, or a smaller starter set (Section 6.2)?
3. Should the understanding step's output ever be visible to the user (e.g., "it looks like you're preparing a pitch — is that right?"), the way clarifying questions are today, or should it operate silently in the common case, matching Conversation Continuity's own transparency-scales-with-stakes principle?

## 10. Outcome Orientation — is Hubi's responsibility an answer, or an accomplished outcome?

A second round of review, added after Section 1–9 above were confirmed. The claim under review here: Hubi's end goal is not an answer, it's a Revenue employee successfully accomplishing real work — and the architecture should be organized around *outcomes* (prepare an onboarding, introduce Wellhub successfully, move an opportunity forward), not just *responses*.

### 10.1 Is there a meaningful distinction between "answer generation" and "work assistance"?

Yes — and it's sharper than the Task Planning distinction already made. Section 4 established *job-to-be-done* (what kind of help is this — draft a pitch, list meetings, prep for negotiation). **Outcome is one level of abstraction deeper: it's the reason the job is worth doing at all.** This is a recognized distinction in product thinking generally (a "job" is the task hired to get done; a "desired outcome" is the success criterion the job is in service of), and the user's own three examples map onto it cleanly: "list the meetings" (job) sits inside "successfully prepare this onboarding" (outcome); "write a pitch" (job) sits inside "successfully introduce Wellhub to this prospect" (outcome).

**A genuine risk that has to be named before this goes any further: this ladder of abstraction doesn't stop at "outcome" on its own — it keeps going.** "Successfully prepare this onboarding" sits inside "retain this client," which sits inside "hit this quarter's quota," which sits inside goals no reasonable system should try to model turn-by-turn. **Outcome has to be explicitly bounded, or this becomes an unfalsifiable, unimplementable idea rather than an architectural concept.** The bound that keeps it tractable: **Outcome is scoped to the business objective of the current work the user is engaged in in this conversation** — not their quota, not their career, not their long-term account strategy. Concretely, this makes Outcome the natural complement to Business/Task Context, not a replacement for it or an open-ended goal-modeling exercise: Context answers "what is this about" (which client, region, segment); Outcome answers "what does success look like in that situation, for this conversation" (onboard this client, land this prospect, advance this opportunity). The two are related but genuinely distinct — two conversations can share identical Business/Task Context (same client, same region) while pursuing different outcomes (one rep is closing the deal, another is just running a scheduled onboarding checklist) — so Outcome has to be established on its own terms, using the same rules already governing Context (explicit statement outranks inference; ask when genuinely unclear), not derived automatically from Context.

### 10.2 Should Hubi be architected around outcomes instead of responses?

**"Instead of" overstates it, and it's worth being precise about why rather than just softening the language.** A response — some artifact, even a plain answer — is still the only mechanism through which Hubi ever actually helps. The user's own diagram still ends in "produce the business artifact"; it doesn't eliminate the response, it adds understanding *ahead* of it. The accurate framing is: **responses become outcome-informed, rather than being determined only by the literal text of the question.** What Outcome actually changes is a prior architectural question that Section 4's Task Planning under-specified: **artifact selection.** "Help me with this Enterprise customer" is close to unclassifiable as a job-to-be-done on its own — it could mean an email, objection handling, a discovery plan, almost anything. It only becomes answerable once an outcome is understood ("move the opportunity forward"), and even then, Hubi may still need to ask *which* artifact would help, exactly as it already asks when Business/Task Context is insufficient. **This means Outcome doesn't only feed Reasoning downstream of Task Planning — for ambiguous requests, understanding the outcome is what makes job classification possible in the first place, not something that happens strictly after it.** The user's own proposed ordering (job → outcome → plan) holds for explicit requests ("write me a pitch," where the job is stated outright); it needs to invert, or resolve as a single joint judgment, for outcome-shaped requests where the job is genuinely unstated. Rather than mandate a fixed sequence, the architecturally honest position is: **job and outcome are resolved together, as an interdependent pair, with the same "ask rather than assume" fallback already governing Business/Task Context** — not a strict pipeline where one always precedes the other.

**A second, equally important correction in the other direction: outcome-awareness must not become license to always produce more than was asked.** Architecture.md's Response Model already states "simple questions should receive simple answers," and Context Builder already exists specifically to "avoid turning simple questions into unnecessarily long workflows." A rep asking a precise policy question mid-call (e.g., a discount threshold) has an outcome behind it too ("get this deal approved") — but the correct artifact is still the precise number, delivered plainly, not a constructed discount-justification narrative Hubi wasn't asked for. Outcome-awareness's job in that case is not to expand the response — it's to inform what, if anything, gets offered *alongside* it. **This is not a new response element to invent: `Architecture.md` Section 11 already documents "Next Step" and "Related Materials" as optional, situational response elements.** Outcome-awareness is what should now drive whether and what those two elements suggest — a foothold that already exists in the documented architecture, just never previously driven by an explicit understanding of outcome.

### 10.3 Does this change the responsibilities of the Conversation Orchestrator, Task Planning, or Reasoning?

- **Task Planning** (Section 4): its output should now be understood as **outcome → job-to-be-done → artifact selection**, resolved as one interdependent judgment rather than three strictly sequential steps (10.2). This is a deepening of what Section 4 already proposed, not an additional new layer — Task Planning was already going to determine job-to-be-done; it now needs to determine the outcome that job serves and, from that, which of the artifact shapes (answer, email, checklist, discovery plan, objection handling, next steps, and so on) actually fits — before Reasoning constructs anything.
- **Conversation Orchestrator** (`Architecture.md` Section 2): its already-documented responsibility to "coordinate the final response structure" now explicitly includes artifact-shape selection informed by outcome, rather than defaulting to a single conversational-answer shape every time. No new responsibility needs to be invented for it — this is a concrete reading of a responsibility it was already assigned.
- **Reasoning** (`Product_Architecture.md` Section 3): its documented definition — "composes a **grounded answer**" — is now measurably narrower than what it needs to describe. This confirms, rather than newly introduces, the naming follow-on already flagged in Section 8 above: "answer" needs to broaden to "artifact" (or "response") in Reasoning's own definition, not only in the terminal box of `Architecture.md`'s high-level diagram. What does **not** change about Reasoning: it still only ever operates on knowledge that has already cleared Applicability, and it still must trace every factual claim inside whatever artifact it constructs back to grounded knowledge — outcome-awareness changes the *shape* of what Reasoning produces, never the *evidentiary standard* it produces it under.

### 10.4 Does this affect the Product Architecture, or is it only an implementation philosophy?

**Neither answer is quite right — the honest answer is that it's already a declared Product Architecture principle, just not where the conceptual reference document currently carries it, and this is worth stating precisely rather than treated as a new idea to adopt.** `CLAUDE.md`'s "Product Thinking" section — the org-level, slow-changing philosophy document, not something invented for this review — already says, verbatim:

> *"Hubi should not optimize for answering more questions. Hubi should optimize for helping Revenue teams accomplish meaningful work... Do not measure success by the amount of information returned. Measure success by the amount of work made easier."*

**This is the exact principle under discussion, already written down, already approved, predating this entire conversation.** What's actually missing is not the principle — it's that `Product_Architecture.md` Section 2's own "Product Principles" list (which sits between the Vision in Section 1 and the concrete mechanisms in Sections 3 onward, and is exactly where standing, foundational commitments like "clarification is preferable to confident but incorrect answers" already live) does not yet explicitly carry this commitment as one of its named principles. **So this doesn't need to become a new principle — it needs to be promoted from `CLAUDE.md` into `Product_Architecture.md`'s own principle list, where it's been implicit in the Vision (Section 1's "prepare for customer conversations," "support decision making") but never stated as a first-class, standing principle of its own.**

This resolves the question cleanly: outcome-orientation is a genuine Product Architecture matter — not merely an implementation philosophy — precisely because it's already the kind of statement `CLAUDE.md` and `Product_Architecture.md` use principles for (a standing commitment that should shape every future decision, not a specific mechanism). Task Planning, artifact selection, and construction latitude (Sections 4, 5, and 10.2–10.3 of this document) are the concrete architectural machinery that make this already-declared principle operative rather than aspirational — this whole three-part review has been building the mechanism for a commitment the project made before any of it started.

### 10.5 Where I disagree, stated plainly

1. **"Outcome-oriented instead of response-oriented" is the one phrase in this round I'd push back on directly.** It reads as eliminating the response as the unit of value, when what's actually being proposed is a response *chosen and shaped by* an understood outcome. The distinction matters because "instead of" could be read as license to skip producing a concrete, gettable artifact in favor of something more abstract — that's not what the examples in this proposal actually describe, and it shouldn't be what gets carried into the principle if it's promoted into `Product_Architecture.md`.
2. **Outcome must be explicitly bounded (10.1), or it isn't an architectural concept at all** — an unbounded "what is this really in service of" question has no natural stopping point short of the user's entire job. Scoping it to "the business objective of the current work in this conversation" is a correction to the proposal, not an endorsement of it as stated.
3. **The proposed strict ordering (job → outcome → plan) doesn't hold for every request (10.2)** — for outcome-shaped, job-ambiguous requests like "help me with this Enterprise customer," treating this as one interdependent judgment with a clarification fallback is more accurate than a fixed pipeline, and more consistent with how Business/Task Context ambiguity is already handled today.
4. **Outcome-awareness must never become a reason to produce more than the user asked for** — this is a real product risk (over-eager, unsolicited elaboration), not a hypothetical one, and it needs to be named as a guardrail alongside the principle itself, not left as an unstated assumption that bigger is more helpful.

## 11. Copilot, Not Agent — Bounding Artifact Inference

A third round, added after Sections 1–10 were confirmed. The claim under review: throughout this review, we've been designing Hubi's ability to infer an outcome and select an artifact — but a copilot must not silently expand the scope of a request into whichever artifact it infers is most valuable. When several artifacts are genuinely plausible, Hubi should expose them as optional continuations ("Would it help if I also prepared...") rather than pick one. Is this a Task Planning concern, a Conversation principle, or a Product principle?

### 11.1 Does the current architecture already guarantee this? — partially, and it matters exactly how

Three anchors already exist, none of them new, and none of them sufficient on their own:

1. **`Architecture.md` Section 11's "Next Step" element already models exactly this pattern, in its own worked example:** *"A practical continuation of the task. Example: 'Would you like me to guide you through the Salesforce opportunity setup?'"* This is structurally identical to the user's own example ("Would it help if I also prepared...") — offered, phrased as a question, never assumed. It was written before this conversation existed.
2. **`Architecture.md` Section 3's original definition of a Business Scenario request** already says the expected behavior is to *"identify the objective; determine which contextual variables are missing; ask relevant follow-up questions"* — not to infer and construct. "I'm meeting Company ABC tomorrow" is a textbook Business Scenario input under this original definition: a situation, not an explicit request, and the documented expected behavior for it was always to ask, not assume.
3. **`CLAUDE.md`'s Core Principle #2, "AI Assists. Humans Decide,"** already states: *"Hubi should never decide on behalf of someone."* As originally written this is scoped to business/commercial decisions (a discount, a package choice) — but the same reasoning extends one level up the stack with no strain at all: deciding *what work to perform* on the user's behalf is the same category of overreach as deciding a business outcome on their behalf. This connection hasn't been made explicit anywhere before now.

**What none of the three anchors do is guarantee the specific behavior being asked for here: when multiple valuable artifacts are plausible, expose the set, not just one suggestion.** "Next Step" as documented is a single, optional suggestion — it was never specified as a menu. And there's a second, more consequential gap: **the last two rounds of this review have been actively building Task Planning's ability to infer outcome and select an artifact — capability this project didn't have before.** That's exactly the kind of new capability that could erode anchors 1–3 if nothing constrains it, not because anything here was designed carelessly, but because more inferential power always needs a matching, explicit boundary, or it will eventually get used past where it should stop. **This is the concrete conceptual change required: Task Planning's output contract needs to be a hard rule, not an implicit expectation** — it must distinguish exactly one **primary** response from zero or more **optional candidates**, and only the primary response may ever be constructed and delivered without being asked for.

### 11.2 The default that resolves the ambiguity

**Primary = the narrowest, most literal, most defensible reading of what was actually said — never the most ambitious inferred reading.** Applied to the user's own examples: *"What meetings do I run during onboarding?"* → primary is the meeting list itself, not a constructed onboarding checklist. *"I'm meeting Company ABC tomorrow"* → there is no explicit request at all, so the primary response is minimal — at most a directly relevant fact if one is obviously and immediately useful, otherwise nothing constructed — with any richer help (a pitch, discovery questions, an account brief) offered, never assumed. This is the same discipline `Architecture.md` Section 11 already states in different words: *"simple questions should receive simple answers."*

**A necessary refinement on the "optional candidates" half, or this fixes one failure mode by creating another:** if Hubi routinely appends a four-item menu to every response, that is not obviously lower cognitive load than a well-chosen default — it can just as easily become its own kind of noise, in tension with the same "avoid unnecessary friction" principle this whole review keeps returning to. Offering has to be **selective and confident** — at most one or two continuations, and only when a specific one is genuinely likely to be valuable given the understood outcome, not a reflexive habit attached to every turn. An "offer" that isn't confident is itself a failure of the same discipline it's meant to enforce.

### 11.3 Resolving the apparent tension with Section 10

Section 10 argued Hubi should optimize for helping accomplish work, not merely answering. Section 11 argues Hubi should not assume what work to do. These sit at different levels, not in contradiction: **"helpful" governs the quality and completeness of the primary response to what was actually asked; "restrained" governs whether Hubi unilaterally expands beyond that into unrequested territory.** A rep who asks for the onboarding meeting list should get a genuinely good, complete answer to that question — Section 10's principle applies in full there. What Section 10 never licensed, and what this section makes explicit, is treating that same question as an invitation to also produce a checklist, a training recommendation, and a set of follow-up questions, unasked.

### 11.4 Answering the three framing questions directly

- **Does this fit naturally within Task Planning?** Partly, and it's worth being precise about which part. Identifying the *candidate set* of plausible artifacts is squarely Task Planning's job — a direct extension of the artifact-selection responsibility Section 10 already assigned it. But confirming which candidates are actually well-supported enough to be worth offering can only happen after retrieval and reasoning have run (an offer backed by nothing real is worse than no offer), and the discipline of *never silently promoting a candidate to delivered content* has to be enforced downstream, in Reasoning and the Grounded Response Builder, not only at the planning stage. So: Task Planning originates the primary/candidate split; Reasoning and the Grounded Response Builder are equally obligated to respect it.
- **Should it become a Conversation principle?** No — and this is worth stating explicitly to avoid a naming collision this project has repeatedly guarded against elsewhere. `Conversation_Continuity_Architecture.md`'s "Conversation" principles govern a different axis entirely: whether prior work across sessions/channels is safe to reattach. This proposal is about scope within a single turn, not continuity across turns or channels. It does have a genuine conversational *shape*, though: an "offer" turn is a close sibling of the clarifying-question turn the runtime already implements (`turnKind`'s existing `CLARIFYING_QUESTION` value) — both are turns where Hubi deliberately doesn't fully resolve the task in one shot and hands part of the decision back to the user, resuming seamlessly next turn via existing session state. That's a structural echo worth noting, not a reason to file this under Conversation Continuity's own vocabulary.
- **Or should it become a Product principle?** Yes. It belongs in `Product_Architecture.md`'s Section 2 principles list, and — matching the pattern from Section 10.4 — it isn't a new idea so much as an explicit extension of `CLAUDE.md`'s existing "AI Assists. Humans Decide," carried one level up the stack from business decisions to task/artifact selection. Recommended, exact wording deferred to you: *"Hubi proposes possible next steps; it does not assume which one the user wants. Reducing effort is not the same as replacing intent."*

### 11.5 A pattern worth naming, across all three rounds

Every capability this review has added has arrived paired with an equally explicit constraint on it, and that pairing is itself the architectural shape worth carrying forward, not just this round's specific conclusion: Task Planning's ability to shape retrieval is paired with the invariant that it may never touch eligibility or applicability (Section 3/7). Reasoning's construction latitude is paired with the claim-level grounding boundary (`docs/response-mode-architecture.md`). Outcome-aware artifact selection is paired with the guardrail against unsolicited elaboration (Section 10.2). And now, Task Planning's ability to infer a candidate artifact set is paired with the requirement that only the narrowest primary response is ever delivered without being asked. **No round of this review has proposed a new capability without a matching boundary in the same breath — that discipline, more than any single mechanism named across these eleven sections, is what should be treated as the stable architecture going forward.**

## 12. Architectural Review

**What this document changes across all three rounds of review:** Round 1 retired Response Mode as an independent concept, repositioned the understanding step before retrieval rather than only before Reasoning, and reframed the new capability as a deepening of Request Classification and the Conversation Orchestrator's existing responsibilities. Round 2 (Section 10) added Outcome as a bounded, explicit concept one level deeper than job-to-be-done, used it to answer the previously-unresolved question of artifact selection, and traced the underlying "help accomplish work, not just answer" principle to its actual origin in `CLAUDE.md`'s existing "Product Thinking" section. Round 3 (Section 11) bounded everything the first two rounds added: it requires Task Planning's output to distinguish exactly one primary response (the narrowest faithful reading of the request) from optional candidates that must be offered, never assumed, and traced that discipline to `CLAUDE.md`'s existing "AI Assists. Humans Decide" principle, extended one level up the stack from business decisions to task selection.

**The standing shape that emerges from all three rounds, stated once, plainly:**

```
User Message
  → Conversation Orchestrator
  → Context Builder            (User / Business-Task / Conversation Context — unchanged)
  → Task Planning               (NEW: outcome → job-to-be-done → primary response + optional
                                  candidate artifacts, resolved jointly, never strictly sequential)
  → Knowledge Engine            (retrieval, now optionally informed by Task Planning's output —
                                  an additional targeted pass, never a replacement for the broad one)
  → Applicability Layer         (unchanged — governs identically regardless of Task Planning's output)
  → Reasoning                   (constructs only the primary response by default; any optional
                                  candidate is offered, never delivered, until the user asks for it;
                                  every factual claim inside whatever it constructs still traces to
                                  grounded knowledge, with zero exception)
  → Grounded Response Builder → Artifact/Answer + optional, selective, confident offers
```

**Where I'm confident:** that the founding vision already supports this direction, in both Rounds 2 and 3 (Sections 10.4 and 11.1 are direct quotations from `CLAUDE.md`, not inferences); that the current flow is exactly retrieve-first with no job-, outcome-, or scope-boundary anywhere (Section 3 is a direct code citation); that Response Mode's lookup-table structure was solving a problem the wrong way round (Section 5); that Outcome needs an explicit bound or it isn't implementable (10.1); that unbounded artifact inference is a real risk this project's own new capability creates and must constrain itself (11.1); and that none of this, across any round, requires or should be allowed to touch the Applicability Layer's authority or the evidentiary standard Reasoning constructs under (Section 7, reaffirmed in 10.3 and 11.2).

**Where this remains a judgment call, not a settled conclusion:** the exact job-to-be-done taxonomy (Section 6.2), whether Decision Support needs splitting (Section 4), the precise wording of both promoted principles in `Product_Architecture.md` (10.4 and 11.4), how many optional candidates is "selective" in practice (11.2 flags the risk but doesn't fix a number — this is a real-usage-tuning question, consistent with `Implementation_Handoff.md` Section 7's own stance that some questions need real testing, not more architecture), and whether any of this is worth its own milestone now versus after the still-not-implemented Findings #001/#002/#003 remediation ships. That sequencing question is a resourcing decision, not an architectural one, and belongs to you.

**This review, across all three rounds, deliberately did not produce an implementation plan, a prompt draft, or a code change.** If this direction — including both principle promotions (10.4, 11.4) — is confirmed, the next step is deciding which of Section 9's open decisions, plus the two principles' exact wording, get resolved first, and only then scoping an implementation plan as its own, separate piece of work.
